\section{Executive Summary}
This thesis addresses a bandwidth bottleneck in wireless neural recording. Bluetooth
Low Energy 6.0 (BLE), the leading low-power off-the-shelf wireless communication protocol, has a theoretical
data rate ceiling of 2~Mbps. Neural action potentials, also called spikes, contain frequencies
from 300~Hz to 6~kHz. Raw extracellular neural electrophysiology data is therefore sampled at 30~kHz
to preserve signal integrity for offline identification of individual
neurons appearing on overlapping recording channels. With a 16-bit resolution, each channel generates 480~kbps, imposing a
theoretical four-channel recording ceiling for wireless transmission. Modern high-density recording
probes, however, carry up to thousands of channels, so transmitting their entire raw data over BLE is
currently impossible. This system instead performs neural identification on-device
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), yielding an
off-the-shelf solution for wireless transmission of putatively identified neurons
compatible with modern rodent neural recording systems.

\section{Problem Identification}

% ======= 1.1 Background and Motivation ======= %
\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Systems neuroscience seeks to explain how the organized interplay of individual neurons
gives rise to sensation, cognition, and behavior~[1]. Answering these questions requires
observing firing activity from multiple neurons across multiple brain regions at once, and studying their relationships.
The neuroscientific community has thus invested heavily in high-density intracranial recording probes. The
Neuropixels~1.0 probe, introduced in 2017, records extracellular activity in rodents from
384 channels simultaneously~[3]. The Neuropixels~2.0 extends recording
capacity to 5120 channels~[4], and Neuropixels 3.0 is currently being developed~[6]. These probes
have already enabled discoveries beyond the reach of single-electrode methods, such as
mapping how choice and action are encoded across the mouse brain~[5]. Furthermore,
high-density extracellular neural recordings employed in model organisms hold promise of
fundamentally changing how the brain, both in health and in disease, is understood.

The quantity high-density intracranial neural recording probes measure is the extracellular action potential, or spike. An
extracellular action potential is the brief voltage deflection an electrically excitable 
cell produces as a function of time when it fires. First
recorded in 1868 by Julius Bernstein~[2], the action potential is the basic unit of neural signaling,
and is the unit of measurement currently available with the highest spatiotemporal neural resolution at scale~[3].
Extracellular spikes contain frequencies from roughly 300~Hz to 6~kHz~[22], so the
industry-standard analog front end digitizes each channel with a 16-bit
analog-to-digital converter (ADC) at 30~kHz for roll-off margin~[7, 8]. At these settings
a full Neuropixels~2.0 quad-base recording generates $5120~\text{channels} \times
30\text{~kS/s} \times 16\text{~bit} \approx 2.46$~Gbps, all of which must leave the
recording probe for downstream spike extraction. The growth in electrode channel count
has far outpaced the wireless bandwidth of low energy communication protocols
that must transmit and receive the neural data.

The questions neural recordings serve are best answered in freely behaving
animals. Wireless headstages have been demonstrated precisely because they free the
animal from its tether and the stress and behavioral limits a cable imposes~[13].
Bluetooth Low Energy 6.0 (BLE) is a state-of-the-art consumer wireless protocol with a
power budget suitable for a head-mounted device~[9, 10]. However, BLE has a theoretical
maximum data rate of 2~Mbps~[9], just $2\text{~Mbps} / 2.46\text{~Gbps} \approx 0.08\%$
of the raw data rate of a
Neuropixels~2.0 quad-base recording. Fortunately, many questions in neuroscientific inquiry 
require only the neural spike signatures, which constitute a small fraction of the continuous 
raw signal~[11, 12]. The proposed project embraces this fact and rethinks how neural 
data is handled in systems neuroscience. By processing the data on-device and transmitting
only the scientifically relevant part of the signal, wirless full-scale, high-density neural recordings are enabled.

% ======= 1.3 Problem Statement ======= %
\subsection{Problem Statement}
Modern neural recording probes give neuroscientists simultaneous access to thousands of neurons in live animals.
The scientific experiments these recordings facilitate would ideally be performed with freely behaving animals. 
Restricting the animals through tethered recordings confounds measurement results, and it strictly limits the possible 
range of experimental paradigms. With thousands of recording channels follows a raw data stream on the order of gigabits
per second, which is three orders of magnitude more data than low-power wireless communication can carry. Thus,
the complete raw data generated by high-density extracellular neural recordings cannot be obtained from freely moving animals.


% ======= 2. Existing Solutions and Previous Work ======= %
\section{Existing Solutions and Previous Work}

% ======= 2.1 Existing Solutions ======= %
\subsection{Existing Solutions}
Two lines of work come close to this project without meeting. One builds wireless
headstages from commodity parts, and the other classifies spikes on the device. Each
addresses only one half of the problem. On the wireless side, headstages made from off-the-shelf parts
already record from freely-moving animals. The simplest stream the raw signal and defer
all detection and sorting to a tethered workstation after the experiment. Tasneem et
al.~[13] send raw data from a moving rat and detect and sort it afterward.
Gagnon-Turcotte et al.~[14] go a step further, pairing an Intan front end with a
Nordic radio to detect and compress spikes on the device, though the sorting into units
still happens at the receiver. In both, the link carries waveforms rather than neural
identities, an approach that still falls orders of magnitude short of the data rates of
state-of-the-art recording probes.

On the other side, on-device classification has been taken furthest in custom silicon.
Fabricated spike-sorting chips reach 85--99\% classification accuracy on the device
itself~[15, 16, 17], which shows the algorithm fits a tight power and area budget. They
are custom parts, however, tested only on replayed recordings and built with no radio,
and producing them requires specialized fabrication facilities not available to typical
neuroscience laboratories.

The same on-device sorting also runs on commodity, programmable hardware. Luan et al.~[18]
perform real-time template matching on an Intan-plus-FPGA headstage in a freely-moving
primate, but log the result to an SD card. Wu et al.~[19] run an unsupervised sorter on an
FPGA at microsecond latency, again with no wireless link. These prove the classification
step is feasible off custom silicon, yet neither closes the wireless half.

The idea of splitting the sorting pipeline between offline training and on-device
classification is not new either. Navajas and Quiroga~[20] showed in simulation that
templates can be built offline and each new spike matched on the device, so that only the
sorted identities travel over the link. What remains undone is to combine these threads.
No published system, to my knowledge, classifies single units on the device and transmits
their identities over a commodity Bluetooth Low Energy link. Combining both halves would
provide neuroscientists with a novel, all-in-one device that allows brain function to be
interrogated with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution.

% ======= 2.2 Proposed Solution =======% 
\subsection{Proposed Solution}
This project proposes a recording head stage that detects spikes and classifies each to a
single unit on-device, transmitting only a unit identity and timestamp per spike
rather than the raw waveform. This design reduces the amount of data per spike by orders
of magnitude, allowing far more channels to be streamed wirelessly than raw transmission
permits while preserving the spatiotemporal spike information that neuroscientific analysis
depends on. Because the design uses only off-the-shelf parts, a laboratory can adopt it
without building custom hardware.

% ======= 2.3 Previous Work ======= %
\subsection{Previous Work}
A custom printed circuit board (PCB) for testing the signal path was designed and built during the summer preceding ES100.
The PCB carries an Intan RHD2164 front end, channel inputs, and SPI communication lines. The board was brought up and used 
to record a single channel from end to end. A pure sine input was passed through the Intan to the nRF54 (SOC) over SPI, 
and across the SoC's Bluetooth Low Energy link to a laptop to prove the signal path. This confirmed the acquisition front 
end that the rest of the system will build on, which was a requirement by my advisor before staring my ES100 project.

\section{Design Requirements and Technical Specifications}

% ======= 3.1 Description and Functional Requirements ======= % 
\subsection{Description and Functional Requirements}
This section states what any valid solution must do, as functional requirements, and the
conditions each behavior must meet, as technical requirements with quantitative
specifications, all stated independently of any particular design. The design approaches
that could realize them are considered in Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:quant}.

Compressing neural data without losing spatiotemporal spike information requires isolating
and preserving exactly the spike events at the required resolution. Any solution must
therefore acquire extracellular voltage from multiple channels (F1), detect spike events in
the acquired signal (F2), log the time of each detected spike (F3), classify each spike and
assign it a unit identity (F4), and transmit each identity with its timestamp over a wireless
link (F5). The technical requirements below are grouped by the functional requirement they
serve.


% ======= 3.2 Technical Requirements and Specifications ======= %
\subsection{Technical Requirements and Specifications}
Each functional requirement imposes one or more technical requirements. Every technical
requirement is given a specification with a quantitative value, and each value is drawn
from a first-principles calculation, from the literature, or from preliminary testing in
this project. The values are set by what neuroscientists need rather than by what a
chosen part happens to provide. Where it is meaningful, a specification therefore lists a
floor the science demands, a target this project commits to, and an aspirational value a
mature device should reach. Some specifications have no meaningful aspirational value.
Sampling faster than the Nyquist rate plus margin adds no scientific content (T2), and the
five-byte packet is already minimal (T9). The aggregate conversion rate and the throughput of the
ADC-to-processor link follow directly from the channel count and the per-channel rate, so
they are verified in testing as derived constraints rather than treated as
specifications. Table~\ref{tab:techreq} summarizes the technical requirements, with T1 through T9 grouped
by the functional requirement they serve and T10 as a system-level power constraint.

% ======= Jusification of Technical Requirements and Specifications ======= %
\subsection{Justification of Technical Requirements and Specifications}
\textbf{Acquisition (F1).} The channel-count values (T1) are set by what neuroscientists
record with, not by any part. Five channels is the floor at which compression demonstrably
does work a raw link cannot. The 32-channel target matches the tetrode microdrives common
in freely-moving rodent studies, and the 384-channel aspiration is one full
Neuropixels~1.0 shank~[3], the scale at which the device would give neuroscientists both
what they want and what they did not know they could have. Extracellular spikes occupy
300~Hz--6~kHz~[22], so the Nyquist--Shannon theorem sets a per-channel floor of
$f_s \geq 2 \times 6\text{~kHz} = 12$~kS/s, and the field-standard
30~kS/s~[7, 8] adds oversampling margin (T2). The resulting aggregate conversion rate and
ADC-to-processor throughput are derived constraints, channels $\times$ rate and channels
$\times$ rate $\times$ bits. They follow arithmetically from T1 and T2, are dictated by
part selection rather than iterated by analysis, and are therefore verified in testing
rather than specified. A 16-bit converter with input-referred noise under
$\sim$5~$\mu$Vrms~[21] resolves the tens-to-hundreds-of-microvolt spikes clear of the
noise floor (T3). A 50~$\mu$V spike against a 5~$\mu$Vrms floor retains a ten-to-one ratio.

\textbf{Detection (F2).} A high-pass corner near 300~Hz separates spikes from the sub-300~Hz
local field potential~[22] (T4). Detection is scored against Kilosort~[24]. On-chip and
commodity detectors report 97--100\%~[14, 15, 16], so a floor of 90\% sensitivity and 90\%
precision, matched within $\pm$1~ms, is conservative yet meaningful, with the
97--100\% of mature detectors as the aspirational reference (T5).

\textbf{Timekeeping (F3).} Preserving spatiotemporal information requires one timestamp per
detected spike, referenced to the acquisition clock (T6).

\textbf{Classification (F4).} Each spike is assigned to one of $K$ units (T7). On-device
accuracy need only beat chance ($1/K$) against Kilosort labels~[24] to prove the pipeline
classifies rather than guesses. Comparable on-chip sorters reach 85--99\%~[15, 16, 17], the
aspirational reference (T8). Both the detection and classification accuracies are genuine
specifications of this design rather than properties inherited from a part. The algorithms
are implemented and iterated on a general-purpose processor as part of this project, not
adopted from an existing spike-sorting chip, so meeting T5 and T8 depends on this
project's own engineering analysis.

\textbf{Transmission (F5).} Transmitting only a one-byte unit identity and a four-byte
timestamp per spike, five bytes, replaces the $40 \times 16\text{~bit} = 80$~bytes of a
raw 40-sample snippet, achieving the
compression the solution depends on (T9).

\textbf{System-level (T10).} A head stage on a freely-moving animal must run on a small
onboard battery, not wall power or a data tether, so its power draw fixes the battery mass
and the length of a recording session. Transmitting only spike identities rather than raw
waveforms cuts the radio's airtime, so the compression that saves bandwidth also saves
transmission energy, and both are quantified in the measurement plan. Should the target be
missed, the design holds three levers, in order of expected saving. They are lengthening the
radio's connection interval, idling the processor between samples, and narrowing the
active channel count, and the measurement plan isolates each contribution so the lever is
chosen from data rather than guesswork. Aspirationally, a multi-day ($\geq$72~h)
session on the same cell is what chronic-recording studies would want and did not know
they could have, and the compression architecture is precisely what makes it
conceivable.

% ======= 4. Plan for Measuring Technical Specifications ======= %
\section{Plan for Measuring Technical Specifications}

The central instrument of the measurement plan is a ground-truth comparison. The same
signal that reaches the nRF54 is split and fed in parallel to a tethered
reference recorder, so the two systems see an identical input. The reference recording is
sorted offline with Kilosort~4.0 to produce ground-truth spike times and unit labels, and the
on-device output is aligned to it through a shared synchronization marker and matched within a
$\pm$1~ms window. When the source is a synthetic recording built from a known file, its labels
serve as the ground truth directly. This split-signal setup is what makes detection (T5) and
classification (T8) measurable against truth rather than against the device's own output.

Because the head stage rides a freely-moving animal, it must run on a small onboard battery, because a
wall supply or a data tether would defeat the wireless purpose of the project. Power draw, and
the radio's share of it in particular, therefore sets the battery mass and the length of a
recording session. Power is measured as supply current times voltage under sustained
operation, with the transmission contribution isolated using a current profiler triggered on
the radio's activity, then converted into battery life for a candidate cell. Transmitting only
spike identities rather than raw waveforms cuts the radio's airtime by orders of magnitude, so
the same on-device classification that saves bandwidth is expected to save transmission energy.
Measuring power with and without on-device classification quantifies that saving (T10).

The remaining specifications are characterized at the bench. Acquisition (T1--T3) uses a
function or arbitrary-waveform generator as the signal source, a logic analyzer on the SPI
bus, and firmware counters. Channel count is read from the acquired stream, the per-channel
rate is confirmed by the spectrum of a known replayed tone and by the sampling-timer period,
and front-end resolution and noise are measured from a shorted input and a known-amplitude
input. The derived throughput constraints are checked alongside by counting sustained and missed
conversions and dropped samples across the ADC-to-processor link. The filter
corner and detection (T4--T5) come from a frequency sweep and from the
$\pm$1~ms match to Kilosort. Timekeeping, classification, and transmission (T6--T9) are read
from the received stream in software. These are one timestamp per event and its jitter against
the sync marker, a confusion matrix of unit assignments against Kilosort labels on held-out
data, and the five-byte content of each packet. Table~\ref{tab:techreq} pairs every specification with its
measurement method alongside its quantitative value.

\subsection{System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:sysdiag} decomposes the proposed head stage into its subsystems and
labels every interface with its physical medium and the data rate it must sustain. The
analog front end amplifies, band-limits, and digitizes the extracellular signal. A
hardware-timed acquisition engine on the system-on-chip (SoC) transfers each sample over
SPI without CPU involvement. The on-device pipeline detects, classifies, and packetizes
spike events, and the radio carries only the resulting five-byte identity-plus-timestamp
packets to the host.

% ======= 5.2 Consideration of Multiple Design Approaches ======= %
\subsection{Consideration of Multiple Design Approaches}\label{sec:approaches}
Five design decisions dominate the system. For each, the feasible approaches are
described with the subsystem interactions they imply and their build and test
consequences. Where two approaches survive this qualitative screen, Section~\ref{sec:quant} evaluates
them quantitatively.

\textbf{Computation platform.} (a)~A commodity BLE system-on-chip of the nRF54 class. The
classifier and the radio share one chip, the front end connects directly over SPI, and
firmware is iterated with ordinary embedded tooling. The acquisition path of this approach
is already built and bench-validated in this project. (b)~An FPGA paired with a separate
radio module. It offers greater parallel throughput at high channel counts, but the pipeline
splits across two chips, adding an inter-chip link whose bandwidth and timing must
themselves be designed and tested, and the build requires an HDL toolchain and a board
respin per iteration. (c)~A custom spike-sorting ASIC. It holds the literature's best power
and accuracy figures~[15, 16, 17], but fabrication requires facilities and timescales
unavailable to a one-year commodity-parts project, and published parts carry no radio.
(d)~Host-side classification. It is trivially feasible, but the link must then carry
waveforms, which is precisely the bandwidth problem this project exists to remove.
Approach~(a) is the only one simultaneously obtainable, programmable, and
radio-integrated, and it is the chosen platform.

\textbf{Sample pacing.} (a)~Software-paced acquisition. A firmware thread issues each
SPI conversion against a deadline. It is simple to build, but the sampling thread and
the BLE controller share the CPU, and the radio's interrupt priority lets it displace
sampling instants. The interaction is measured, not hypothetical. (b)~Hardware-timed
acquisition. A timer peripheral triggers each SPI transaction through the SoC's
event-routing fabric with DMA collecting results, removing the CPU, and therefore the
radio, from the timing path at the cost of more involved peripheral bring-up. Both were
built and measured on identical hardware in this project, and Section~\ref{sec:quant} reports the
comparison.

\textbf{Acquisition bus topology.} (a)~A single SPI bus. One front end carries all
channels on four wires, with the simplest layout and firmware, but every channel shares
one measured conversion-rate ceiling. (b)~Dual front ends on dual buses. The ceiling
doubles and the radio-facing pipeline is unchanged (packets merge in memory), at the
cost of a second front end, doubled routing, and a second DMA engine. The choice gates
the custom PCB layout, and Section~\ref{sec:quant} quantifies both against the T1/T2 targets.

\textbf{Classification algorithm.} This decision defines the system, and the two approaches
differ in where the knowledge about the neurons comes from. (a)~Offline-supervised
template matching. A tethered reference recording is sorted offline with Kilosort, one
template is built per unit, and the templates are quantized and flashed to the device. Each
detected spike is then assigned to the nearest template by Euclidean distance~[20]. The host
supplies the model and the device never changes it, so unit identities stay fixed and the
confusion matrix against Kilosort labels measures T8 directly. The cost is a per-subject
offline phase before every wireless session, and templates that go stale as the electrode
drifts. (b)~Online unsupervised sorting with OSort~[25]. The device starts with no model.
Each spike is compared with the running mean of every existing cluster, joins the nearest
one and updates its mean if the distance is below a noise-derived threshold, and otherwise
opens a new cluster. Clusters that come closer than the threshold are merged. There is no
offline phase and the sorter follows electrode drift, which is why the fabricated on-chip
sorters~[15, 16] and the FPGA sorter of Wu et al.~[19] all build on it. The price is paid
elsewhere in the system. Cluster identities are assigned on the device, so the radio
protocol must also carry cluster creation and merge events, and validation requires
matching device clusters to Kilosort units after the fact instead of reading a confusion
matrix off fixed labels. The per-spike arithmetic is the same nearest-template distance in
both approaches, so the device compute budget does not decide between them. Template
matching is the primary approach for this project because its accuracy can be measured
cleanly against ground truth, which is what T8 requires. OSort is the alternative the
design keeps open, and it is the natural path to the multi-day sessions of the T10
aspiration, where drift would defeat a static template.

\textbf{Delivery path for bench validation.} (a)~Direct BLE from the head stage to the
host laptop. It needs no additional hardware, but the laptop operating system's
connection-interval floor caps the raw-streaming rate used during validation. (b)~An
nRF-to-nRF relay, a second development kit forwarding to the host over wired serial. It
adds one off-the-shelf board and recovers the radio's full measured throughput. This
decision affects only the validation bench.

\section{Quantitative Evaluation of Design Approaches}\label{sec:quant}

\subsection{Software-Paced versus Hardware-Timed Sample Pacing}
Both pacing approaches of Section~\ref{sec:approaches} were built and measured with the same
instrument, an oscilloscope on the front end's chip-select line. Every falling edge is one
conversion request, so the distribution of edge-to-edge intervals is the sampling regularity
itself, measured independently of the device under test. About
12{,}800 intervals were captured per approach at 20~kS/s nominal (50~$\mu$s period) with
the BLE link streaming. Table~\ref{tab:pacing}
summarizes the result. The software-paced sampler, displaced by the radio's
interrupt-priority work, placed only 0.7\% of samples within $\pm$10\% of the nominal
period, while the hardware-timed engine placed 100.0\% of them, a 62$\times$ reduction
in standard deviation. Averages alone would not have distinguished the two, so regularity was measured at the
signal level rather than inferred from throughput. The hardware-timed engine is the chosen
approach. Its two residuals are understood. The mean reads $+0.34$\% because the timer runs
from an uncalibrated on-chip oscillator, and the one $\sim$53~$\mu$s interval per 1.64~s is
the deliberate buffer-rewind pause. Figure~\ref{fig:pacinghist} overlays the
two measured interval distributions on a shared axis.

\subsection{Single versus Dual SPI Bus Topology}
The acquisition ceiling of one SPI bus was measured directly, using the bench-validated
settings of a 21.33~MHz clock, hardware chip-select, and 16-bit words. The bus sustains
757{,}741 conversions per second, one word per 1.32~$\mu$s of bus time. At the
32-channel target of T1, a single bus offers
$757{,}741 / 32 \approx 23.7$~kS/s per channel, above the 12~kS/s Nyquist floor of T2
but 21\% short of the 30~kS/s target. Conversely, at full 30~kS/s a single bus carries
at most 25 channels. A dual bus doubles the ceiling to $\approx$1.52~M~words/s,
47.4~kS/s per channel at 32 channels, meeting the T1 and T2 targets with 58\% margin. The single bus therefore meets the floors and
the dual bus the targets. Because the choice sets the number of front ends and the board
routing, it must be finalized before layout, with the dual bus as the default unless the
power budget argues otherwise.

\subsection{Offline Templates versus Online OSort for Classification}
Both classification approaches of Section~\ref{sec:approaches} reduce to the same per-spike
operation, a nearest-template distance over $K$ stored waveforms of $N$ samples, so their
cost on the device can be computed directly. With $N = 40$ and $K = 8$, one spike costs
320 multiply-accumulates. At 32 channels and 40 spikes per second per channel, about
1{,}300 spikes per second, that is 0.4 million multiply-accumulates per second against the
nRF54's 128~MHz Cortex-M33 core, under one percent of its capacity. Static templates occupy $K \times N \times 2$ bytes, 640~bytes per channel, and the FPGA OSort of
Wu et al.\ runs in 256~bytes per channel~[19], so compute and memory do not separate the
two approaches.

Accuracy does, and it is bounded differently for each. Two static templates separated by a
Euclidean distance $d$ under per-sample noise $\sigma$ are confused with probability
$\Phi(-d/2\sigma)$. With the 5~$\mu$Vrms of T3 and templates that differ by only 5~$\mu$V
per sample over 40 samples, $d \approx 32~\mu$V and the error is about 0.1\%. Template matching is
therefore limited by electrode drift and overlapping spikes, which a template fitted
offline cannot follow, rather than by noise. Online sorting follows drift by construction
but is bounded by cluster stability, and the published figures reflect that. The adaptive on-chip sorters report
72 to 98\% depending on the dataset~[15], 85 to 94\%~[16], and 80 to 96\% with an in-vivo
average of 79.8\%~[19]. No quantity available today separates the two approaches
decisively, so the choice rests on T8, which requires accuracy measured against fixed
Kilosort labels and favors the offline templates. OSort remains the path for sessions long
enough for drift to matter, the T10 aspiration, and the decisive comparison on this
project's own signals is scheduled in Table~\ref{tab:timeline} with the same
confusion-matrix method for both.
