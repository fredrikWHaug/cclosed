\section{Executive Summary}
This thesis addresses a bandwidth bottleneck in wireless neural recording. Bluetooth
Low Energy 6.0 (BLE), the leading low-power off-the-shelf wireless communication protocol, has a theoretical
data rate ceiling of 2~Mbps. Neural action potentials, also called spikes, contain frequencies
from 300~Hz to 6~kHz. Raw extracellular neural electrophysiology data is therefore sampled at 30~kHz
to preserve signal integrity for offline identification of individual
neurons appearing on overlapping recording channels. With a 16-bit resolution, each channel generates 480~kbps, imposing a
theoretical four-channel recording ceiling for wireless transmission. Modern high-density recording
probes, however, carry up to thousands of channels, so transmitting their entire raw data over BLE is
currently impossible. This system instead performs neural identification on-device
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}), yielding an
off-the-shelf solution for wireless transmission of putatively identified neurons
compatible with modern rodent neural recording systems. Beyond the proposal, this
checkpoint adds the design approach: the system architecture, consideration of
alternative designs with quantitative evaluations grounded in this project's bench
measurements, and the training, timeline, and budget plans.

\section{Problem Identification}

% ======= 1.1 Background and Motivation ======= %
\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Systems neuroscience seeks to explain how the organized interplay of individual neurons
gives rise to sensation, cognition, and behavior~[1]. Answering such questions requires
observing many neurons at once, across anatomically defined brain regions. The field
has therefore invested heavily in high-density intracranial recording probes. The
Neuropixels~1.0 probe, introduced in 2017, records extracellular activity in rodents on
384 channels simultaneously~[3]. The Neuropixels~2.0 extends recording
capacity to 5120 channels~[4], and a third generation is in development~[6]. These probes
have already enabled discoveries beyond the reach of single-electrode methods, such as
mapping how choice and action are encoded across the entire mouse brain~[5]. Furthermore,
high-density extracellular neural recordings employed in model organisms hold promise of
fundamentally changing how the brain, both in health and in disease, is understood.

The quantity these probes measure is the extracellular action potential, or spike: the
brief voltage deflection an electrically excitable cell produces when it fires. First
recorded in 1868 by Julius Bernstein using a differential rheotome of his own
invention~[2], the action potential remains the basic unit of neural signaling, and the
interplay between neurons is studied by measuring the spikes of individual cells~[3].
Extracellular spikes contain frequencies from roughly 300~Hz to 6~kHz~[22], so the
industry-standard analog front end digitizes each channel with a 16-bit
analog-to-digital converter (ADC) at 30~kHz for roll-off margin~[7, 8]. At these settings
a full Neuropixels~2.0 quad-base recording generates $5120~\text{channels} \times
30\text{~kS/s} \times 16\text{~bit} \approx 2.46$~Gbps, all of which must leave the
animal's head to be analyzed. The growth in channel count, and with it in the volume of
data each probe generates, has far outpaced the bandwidth of the communication hardware
that must carry that data. Within the full recording chain, from implanted probe through
head-mounted electronics to the computers where analysis runs, this project resides at
the head-mounted stage and its outbound data link.

The questions these recordings serve are, moreover, best answered in freely behaving
animals, and wireless headstages have been demonstrated precisely because they free the
animal from its tether and the stress and behavioral limits a cable imposes~[13].
Bluetooth Low Energy 6.0 (BLE) is a state-of-the-art consumer wireless protocol with a
power budget suitable for a head-mounted device~[9, 10]. However, BLE has a theoretical
maximum data rate of 2~Mbps~[9], just $2\text{~Mbps} / 2.46\text{~Gbps} \approx 0.08\%$
of the raw data rate of a
Neuropixels~2.0 quad-base recording. Fortunately, many questions in neuroscientific inquiry 
require only the neural spike signatures, which constitute a small fraction of the continuous 
raw signal~[11, 12]. This project embraces these technical developments and reexamines how neural 
data is handled in systems neuroscience: process the data on the device itself, and transmit
only the scientifically relevant part of the signal.

% ======= 1.2 Stakeholders, Client, and End Used of the Solution ======= %
\subsection{Stakeholders, Client, and End User of the Solution}
Several groups have a stake in the project (Figure~\ref{fig:stakeholders}). The animals
that carry the device benefit most directly. Neuroscientists gain because
recording more channels wirelessly allows experiments that are impractical today, and the
agencies that fund their work share that gain. Preclinical drug researchers have an
interest too, since studies of the central nervous system often depend on recordings from
animals that move freely.

\centering

\caption{Stakeholder map. Proximity to the center encodes directness of stake, from the

The client is the neuroscientist whose laboratory would buy the device and use it in its
own work, whether a single academic group or a shared institutional facility.

The end users are systems neuroscientists. They study how neural activity relates to
behavior, which works best when the animal can move freely. Standard high-density recording
ties the animal to a cable that carries the raw data. This limits movement and rules out
experiments such as social interaction. A headstage that transmits wirelessly removes that
limit and lets researchers run a wider range of experiments.

% ======= 1.3 Problem Statement ======= %
\subsection{Problem Statement}
Modern recording probes give neuroscientists simultaneous access to thousands of neurons,
and the questions those recordings serve are best answered while the animal behaves
naturally. Every recorded sample, however, must leave the animal's head to be analyzed,
and at thousands of channels the raw stream is measured in gigabits per
second, roughly a thousand times more than low-power wireless technology can carry.
Experimenters are therefore forced to choose between recording at scale and letting the
animal move freely. A solution must deliver the scientifically necessary content of a
high-density recording without a cable, within the power budget of a head-mounted
device.


% ======= 2. Existing Solutions and Previous Work ======= %
\section{Existing Solutions and Previous Work}

% ======= 2.1 Existing Solutions ======= %
\subsection{Existing Solutions}
Two lines of work come close to this project without meeting: wireless headstages built
from commodity parts, and systems that classify spikes on the device. Each addresses only
one half of the problem. On the wireless side, headstages made from off-the-shelf parts
already record from freely-moving animals. The simplest stream the raw signal and defer
all detection and sorting to a tethered workstation after the experiment; Tasneem et
al.~[13] send raw data from a moving rat and detect and sort it afterward.
Gagnon-Turcotte et al.~[14] go a step further, pairing an Intan front end with a
Nordic radio to detect and compress spikes on the device, though the sorting into units
still happens at the receiver. In both, the link carries waveforms rather than neural
identities, an approach that still falls orders of magnitude short of the data rates of
state-of-the-art recording probes.

On the other side, on-device classification has been taken furthest in custom silicon.
Fabricated spike-sorting chips reach 85--99\% classification accuracy on the device
itself~[15, 16, 17], which shows the algorithm fits a tight power and area budget. They
are custom parts, however, tested only on replayed recordings and built with no radio,
and producing them requires specialized fabrication facilities not available to typical
neuroscience laboratories.

The same on-device sorting also runs on commodity, programmable hardware. Luan et al.~[18]
perform real-time template matching on an Intan-plus-FPGA headstage in a freely-moving
primate, but log the result to an SD card. Wu et al.~[19] run an unsupervised sorter on an
FPGA at microsecond latency, again with no wireless link. These prove the classification
step is feasible off custom silicon, yet neither closes the wireless half.

The idea of splitting the sorting pipeline between offline training and on-device
classification is not new either. Navajas and Quiroga~[20] showed in simulation that
templates can be built offline and each new spike matched on the device, so that only the
sorted identities travel over the link. What remains undone is to combine these threads.
No published system, to my knowledge, classifies single units on the device and transmits
their identities over a commodity Bluetooth Low Energy link. Combining both halves would
provide neuroscientists with a novel, all-in-one device that allows brain function to be
interrogated with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution.

% ======= 2.2 Proposed Solution =======% 
\subsection{Proposed Solution}
This project proposes a recording head stage that detects spikes and classifies each to a
single unit on-device, transmitting only a unit identity and timestamp per spike
rather than the raw waveform. This design reduces the amount of data per spike by orders
of magnitude, allowing far more channels to be streamed wirelessly than raw transmission
permits while preserving the spatiotemporal spike information that neuroscientific analysis
depends on. Because the design uses only off-the-shelf parts, a laboratory can adopt it
without building custom hardware.

% ======= 2.3 Previous Work ======= %
\subsection{Previous Work}
Only one part of the system has been built so far: a custom circuit board for testing the
signal path. It carries an Intan RHD2164 front end wired to an
nRF54LM20B over SPI. The board has been brought up and used to record a single channel from
end to end, with a known input passing through the Intan, across the SoC's Bluetooth Low
Energy link, and out to a laptop. This confirms the acquisition front end that the rest of
the system will build on.

\subsection{Description and Functional Requirements}
This section decomposes the problem statement into the behaviors any valid solution must
exhibit (the functional requirements) and the qualitative conditions each behavior must
satisfy (the technical requirements). Each technical requirement is then made concrete as
a technical specification: a measurable parameter paired with a target value. These
requirements and specifications are stated independently of any particular design; the
design approaches that could realize them are considered in Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:quant}.

Compressing continuous neural data without fidelity loss of spatiotemporal spike
information requires isolating and preserving exactly the neural spike events at the
required resolution. Any solution to the problem statement must therefore
perform five operations: it must acquire extracellular voltage from multiple
recording channels, detect spike events within the acquired signal, log the
time at which each detected spike occurs, classify each detected spike and assign it
a unit identity, and transmit each unit identity coupled with its timestamp over a
wireless link. These five behaviors constitute the functional requirements of the system
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:funcreq}.

% ======= 3.2 Technical Requirements and Specifications ======= %
\subsection{Technical Requirements and Specifications}
Each functional requirement imposes one or more technical requirements, and each
requirement's specification draws its value from a first-principles calculation, the
prior-art literature, or preliminary testing conducted for this project. Two principles
govern how the values are set. First, a good specification is set by what the end user
needs, not by what a chosen part happens to provide: where meaningful, each specification
therefore states a floor the science demands, a target this project commits to, and an
aspirational value reflecting what neuroscientists would ask of a mature device. Second,
quantities fixed by part selection rather than by engineering analysis are treated as
derived constraints to be verified in testing, not as independent specifications; of this
kind are the aggregate conversion rate and the ADC-to-processor link throughput, each of
which follows arithmetically from the channel count and per-channel rate. The technical
requirements are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:techreq}: T1 through T9 grouped by the
functional requirement each serves, and T10 a system-level power constraint.
Justifications follow the table.

% ======= Jusification of Technical Requirements and Specifications ======= %
\subsection{Justification of Technical Requirements and Specifications}
\textbf{Acquisition (F1).} The channel-count values (T1) are set by what neuroscientists
record with, not by any part. Five channels is the floor at which compression demonstrably
does work a raw link cannot. The 32-channel target matches the tetrode microdrives common
in freely-moving rodent studies, and the 384-channel aspiration is one full
Neuropixels~1.0 shank~[3], the scale at which the device would give neuroscientists both
what they want and what they did not know they could have. Extracellular spikes occupy
300~Hz--6~kHz~[22], so the Nyquist--Shannon theorem sets a per-channel floor of
$f_s \geq 2 \times 6\text{~kHz} = 12$~kS/s, and the field-standard
30~kS/s~[7, 8] adds oversampling margin (T2). The resulting aggregate conversion rate and
ADC-to-processor throughput are derived constraints (channels $\times$ rate, and channels
$\times$ rate $\times$ bits): they follow arithmetically from T1 and T2, are dictated by
part selection rather than iterated by analysis, and are therefore verified in testing
rather than specified. A 16-bit converter with input-referred noise under
$\sim$5~$\mu$Vrms~[21] resolves the tens-to-hundreds-of-microvolt spikes clear of the
noise floor (T3): a 50~$\mu$V spike against a 5~$\mu$Vrms floor retains a 10:1 ratio.

\textbf{Detection (F2).} A high-pass corner near 300~Hz separates spikes from the sub-300~Hz
local field potential~[22] (T4). Detection is scored against Kilosort~[24]: since on-chip and
commodity detectors report 97--100\%~[14, 15, 16], a floor of 90\% sensitivity and 90\%
precision, matched within $\pm$1~ms, is conservative yet meaningful (T5).

\textbf{Timekeeping (F3).} Preserving spatiotemporal information requires one timestamp per
detected spike, referenced to the acquisition clock (T6).

\textbf{Classification (F4).} Each spike is assigned to one of $K$ units (T7). On-device
accuracy need only beat chance ($1/K$) against Kilosort labels~[24] to prove the pipeline
classifies rather than guesses; comparable on-chip sorters reach 85--99\%~[15, 16, 17], the
aspirational reference (T8). Both the detection and classification accuracies are genuine
specifications of this design rather than properties inherited from a part: the algorithms
are implemented and iterated on a general-purpose processor as part of this project, not
adopted from an existing spike-sorting chip, so meeting T5 and T8 depends on this
project's own engineering analysis.

\textbf{Transmission (F5).} Transmitting only a one-byte unit identity and a four-byte
timestamp per spike, five bytes, replaces the $40 \times 16\text{~bit} = 80$~bytes of a
raw 40-sample snippet, achieving the
compression the solution depends on (T9).

\textbf{System-level (T10).} A head stage on a freely-moving animal must run on a small
onboard battery, not wall power or a data tether, so its power draw fixes the battery mass
and the length of a recording session. Transmitting only spike identities rather than raw
waveforms cuts the radio's airtime, so the compression that saves bandwidth also saves
transmission energy; both are quantified in the measurement plan. Should the target be
missed, the design holds identified levers, in order of expected saving: lengthening the
radio's connection interval, idling the processor between samples, and narrowing the
active channel count; the measurement plan isolates each contribution so the lever is
chosen from data rather than guesswork.

% ======= 4. Plan for Measuring Technical Specifications ======= %
\section{Plan for Measuring Technical Specifications}

The central instrument of the measurement plan is a ground-truth comparison. The same
signal that reaches the nRF54 is split and fed in parallel to a tethered
reference recorder, so the two systems see an identical input. The reference recording is
sorted offline with Kilosort~4.0 to produce ground-truth spike times and unit labels, and the
on-device output is aligned to it through a shared synchronization marker and matched within a
$\pm$1~ms window. When the source is a synthetic recording built from a known file, its labels
serve as the ground truth directly. This split-signal setup is what makes detection (T5) and
classification (T8) measurable against truth rather than against the device's own output, and
it is the same architecture a later live-animal study would reuse.

Because the head stage rides a freely-moving animal, it must run on a small onboard battery; a
wall supply or a data tether would defeat the wireless purpose of the project. Power draw, and
the radio's share of it in particular, therefore sets the battery mass and the length of a
recording session. Power is measured as supply current times voltage under sustained
operation, with the transmission contribution isolated using a current profiler triggered on
the radio's activity, then converted into battery life for a candidate cell. Transmitting only
spike identities rather than raw waveforms cuts the radio's airtime by orders of magnitude, so
the same on-device classification that saves bandwidth is expected to save transmission energy;
measuring power with and without on-device classification quantifies that saving (T10).

The remaining specifications are characterized at the bench. Acquisition (T1--T3) uses a
function or arbitrary-waveform generator as the signal source, a logic analyzer on the SPI
bus, and firmware counters: channel count is read from the acquired stream, the per-channel
rate is confirmed by the spectrum of a known replayed tone and by the sampling-timer period,
and front-end resolution and noise are measured from a shorted input and a known-amplitude
input. The derived throughput constraints are checked alongside: sustained and missed
conversions and dropped samples are counted across the ADC-to-processor link. The filter
corner and detection (T4--T5) come from a frequency sweep and from the
$\pm$1~ms match to Kilosort. Timekeeping, classification, and transmission (T6--T9) are read
from the received stream in software: one timestamp per event and its jitter against the sync
marker, a confusion matrix of unit assignments against Kilosort labels on held-out data, and
the five-byte content of each packet. Table~\ref{tab:techreq} pairs every specification with its
measurement method alongside its quantitative value.

\subsection{System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:sysdiag} decomposes the proposed head stage into its subsystems and
labels every interface with its physical medium and the data rate it must sustain. The
analog front end amplifies, band-limits, and digitizes the extracellular signal; a
hardware-timed acquisition engine on the system-on-chip (SoC) transfers each sample over
SPI without CPU involvement; the on-device pipeline detects, classifies, and packetizes
spike events; and the radio carries only the resulting five-byte identity-plus-timestamp
packets to the host. The battery powers the two head-mounted components.

% ======= 5.2 Consideration of Multiple Design Approaches ======= %
\subsection{Consideration of Multiple Design Approaches}\label{sec:approaches}
Five design decisions dominate the system. For each, the feasible approaches are
described with the subsystem interactions they imply and their build and test
consequences; where two approaches survive this qualitative screen, Section~\ref{sec:quant} evaluates
them quantitatively.

\textbf{Computation platform.} (a)~A commodity BLE system-on-chip (nRF54 class): the
classifier and the radio share one chip, the front end connects directly over SPI, and
firmware is iterated with ordinary embedded tooling; the acquisition path of this
approach is already built and bench-validated in this project. (b)~An FPGA paired with a
separate radio module: greater parallel throughput at high channel counts, but the
pipeline splits across two chips, adding an inter-chip link whose bandwidth and timing
must themselves be designed and tested, and the build requires an HDL toolchain and a
board respin per iteration. (c)~A custom spike-sorting ASIC: the literature's best power
and accuracy figures~[15, 16, 17], but fabrication requires facilities and timescales
unavailable to a one-year commodity-parts project, and published parts carry no radio.
(d)~Host-side classification: trivially feasible, but the link must then carry
waveforms, which is precisely the bandwidth problem this project exists to remove.
Approach~(a) is the only one simultaneously obtainable, programmable, and
radio-integrated; it is the chosen platform.

\textbf{Sample pacing.} (a)~Software-paced acquisition: a firmware thread issues each
SPI conversion against a deadline. It is simple to build, but the sampling thread and
the BLE controller share the CPU, and the radio's interrupt priority lets it displace
sampling instants; the interaction is measured, not hypothetical. (b)~Hardware-timed
acquisition: a timer peripheral triggers each SPI transaction through the SoC's
event-routing fabric with DMA collecting results, removing the CPU, and therefore the
radio, from the timing path at the cost of more involved peripheral bring-up. Both were
built and measured on identical hardware in this project; Section~\ref{sec:quant} reports the
comparison.

\textbf{Acquisition bus topology.} (a)~A single SPI bus: one front end carries all
channels on four wires, with the simplest layout and firmware, but every channel shares
one measured conversion-rate ceiling. (b)~Dual front ends on dual buses: the ceiling
doubles and the radio-facing pipeline is unchanged (packets merge in memory), at the
cost of a second front end, doubled routing, and a second DMA engine. The choice gates
the custom PCB layout; Section~\ref{sec:quant} quantifies both against the T1/T2 targets.

\textbf{Classification algorithm.} All candidates occupy the same place in the pipeline,
between detection and packetization; they differ in what must be computed per spike on
the device and what the host must supply beforehand. (a)~Template matching: unit
templates are built offline from a reference recording and each detected spike is
assigned to the nearest template on the device~[20]; the per-spike cost is a handful of
multiply-accumulates per template, well within the SoC's budget, and accuracy is directly
scorable as a confusion matrix against Kilosort labels. (b)~On-device feature extraction
with unsupervised clustering~[19]: no offline phase, but higher memory and compute, and
its evolving cluster identities are harder to validate against fixed ground truth.
(c)~A small quantized neural network: comparable inference cost to templates but a
heavier training and deployment pipeline, retrained per session. Template matching is
the primary approach, matching the offline/online split of the prior art; the network
remains a documented fallback if template accuracy plateaus below the T8 target.

\textbf{Delivery path for bench validation.} (a)~Direct BLE from the head stage to the
host laptop: no additional hardware, but the laptop operating system's connection-interval
floor caps the raw-streaming rate used during validation. (b)~An nRF-to-nRF relay
(second development kit forwarding to the host over wired serial): adds one
off-the-shelf board and recovers the radio's full measured throughput. This decision
affects only the validation bench; the final system's per-spike packets sit orders of
magnitude below either ceiling.

\section{Quantitative Evaluation of Design Approaches}\label{sec:quant}

\subsection{Sample Pacing: Software-Paced versus Hardware-Timed}
Both pacing approaches of Section~\ref{sec:approaches} were built in this project and measured with the
same instrument: an oscilloscope on the front end's chip-select line, where every falling
edge is one conversion request, so the distribution of edge-to-edge intervals is the
sampling regularity itself, measured independently of the device under test. Roughly
12{,}800 consecutive intervals were captured per approach at a 20~kS/s nominal rate
(50~$\mu$s period) with the BLE link streaming throughout. Table~\ref{tab:pacing}
summarizes the result: the software-paced sampler, displaced by the radio's
interrupt-priority work, placed only 0.7\% of samples within $\pm$10\% of the nominal
period, while the hardware-timed engine placed 100.0\% of them, a 62$\times$ reduction
in standard deviation. Averages alone would not have distinguished the two (both hold
the nominal rate), which is why regularity was measured at the signal level rather than
inferred from throughput. The hardware-timed engine is the chosen approach; its two
residuals are understood and bounded (the mean reads $+0.34$\% because the timer runs
from an uncalibrated on-chip oscillator, and one $\sim$53~$\mu$s interval per 1.64~s is
the engine's deliberate buffer-rewind pause).

\subsection{Bus Topology: Single versus Dual SPI Bus}
The acquisition ceiling of one SPI bus was measured directly, using the bench-validated
settings of a 21.33~MHz clock, hardware chip-select, and 16-bit words: the bus sustains
757{,}741 conversions per second, i.e.\ one word per 1.32~$\mu$s of bus time. The
channel arithmetic follows: at the 32-channel target of T1, a single bus offers
$757{,}741 / 32 \approx 23.7$~kS/s per channel, above the 12~kS/s Nyquist floor of T2
but 21\% short of the 30~kS/s target; conversely, at full 30~kS/s a single bus carries
at most 25 channels. A dual-bus topology doubles the measured ceiling to
$\approx$1.52~M~words/s, which at 32 channels allows 47.4~kS/s per channel, meeting the
T1 and T2 targets with 58\% margin. The single bus therefore satisfies the
specification floors and the dual bus the targets; because the choice sets the number of
front ends and the routing of the custom circuit board, it must be finalized before
layout, with the dual bus as the default unless the remaining power budget argues
otherwise.

\subsection{Link Budget: Raw Waveforms versus Spike Identities}
The core design claim is evaluated analytically. Raw transmission scales with channel
count and sample rate: $32 \times 30\text{~kS/s} \times 16\text{~bit} = 15.36$~Mbps,
7.7$\times$ the radio's 2~Mbps theoretical ceiling, which is why raw transmission caps
at four channels. Spike-identity transmission instead scales with firing rate: at a
conservatively high sustained rate of 40 spikes per second per channel, $32 \times 40
\times 5\text{~B} = 51.2$~kbps, a $300\times$ reduction, occupying 2.6\% of the radio's
ceiling. The crossover at which spike-identity transmission stops winning is a firing
rate of $480{,}000 / 40 = 12{,}000$ spikes per second per channel, two orders of
magnitude beyond physiological rates, so the advantage is robust to any realistic spike
activity. Equivalently, within 2~Mbps the radio can carry the identities of
$2\text{~Mbps} / 40\text{~bit} = 50{,}000$ spikes per second, which at 40~spikes/s per
unit is over a thousand units, against four channels of raw waveform.
